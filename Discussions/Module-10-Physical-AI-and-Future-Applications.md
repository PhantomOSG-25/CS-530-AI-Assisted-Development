# Module 10 Discussion: Reflecting on Physical AI and Future Applications

One physical AI application that stands out to me is the use of automated guided vehicles, or AGVs, in manufacturing. At Hankook Tire, where I currently work, we have automated material-moving vehicles that we refer to as AGVs, so this is something I actually see in a working manufacturing environment rather than only reading about it. AGVs and other autonomous robots are already being used in manufacturing for transportation, material handling, and other repetitive tasks, making them an important part of Industry 4.0 systems (Teixeira et al., 2023). Computer vision can support this type of physical AI by helping a robot interpret its surroundings, recognize obstacles, and operate around people and equipment. Natural language processing is not something I necessarily see being used by the AGVs at Hankook today, but I can see it becoming more important as robotics advances. Research into LLM-based robotics shows that combining language understanding with visual perception can allow robots to understand human instructions, plan tasks, and interact more naturally with people (Raptis et al., 2025). Over the next five years, I expect systems like AGVs to become more flexible and capable of adjusting to changing factory conditions instead of relying as heavily on fixed routes or predetermined instructions.

The biggest concern for me is safety. A mistake made by software on a computer screen may cause an error message or incorrect result, but a mistake made by a physical AI system operating around people can cause an injury. Working at Hankook has made me look at this differently because quality and safety are taken seriously in tire manufacturing. We are producing tires that will eventually be on vehicles carrying people and their families, so quality has to come first because lives can depend on the product working correctly. I believe the same standard should apply to physical AI. Efficiency and productivity are important, but they should never come before safety. Raptis et al. (2025) identify safety, human oversight, transparency, and accountability as major concerns as autonomous robotic systems become more capable. I think humans still need the ability to monitor these systems, stop them when necessary, and verify that they are operating correctly instead of assuming that automation is automatically safe.

This course has also helped me see how AI connects to the technology career I am working toward. I have learned more about prompt engineering, neural networks, training data, hallucinations, bias, security, testing, and human oversight. One of the biggest lessons I am taking from the course is that an AI system sounding confident or producing something that appears to work does not prove that it is correct. Seeing physical automation at Hankook reinforces that lesson because there is a much smaller margin for error when software controls something moving in the real world. As I continue moving toward roles in application support, software testing, systems analysis, QA, and eventually more advanced development work, I think understanding both the benefits and limitations of AI will help me troubleshoot systems, question unexpected results, test more carefully, and use AI as a tool without depending on it to make decisions for me.
