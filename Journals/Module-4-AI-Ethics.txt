Ethical Principles and Implementation

       Artificial intelligence can improve how people work, solve problems, and make

decisions, but I do not believe that means it should operate without limits. The ethical principles

that matter most to me are fairness, accountability, transparency, human oversight, privacy, and

safety. I also believe those principles should apply across the board, no matter what field or

industry is using AI.

       Huang et al. (2023) point out that AI can create concerns involving privacy,

discrimination, unemployment, safety, and security. Before any AI system is released, I believe it

should be thoroughly tested for bias and other problems. It should be grounded in verifiable facts

instead of made-up information, important decisions should require human review, and there

should be detailed documentation so a user or administrator can go back and see how a response

or decision was reached. Monitoring should not stop after release either. AI systems need to be

watched and tested as long as they are being used.

Technological Safeguards

       Technology can help enforce these principles, but I do not trust a safeguard just because

someone says it is there. Bias testing is a must, documentation is non-negotiable, human

approval should be required for important decisions, and the system should be constantly

monitored. Huang et al. (2023) explain that explainable AI can help people understand how an

AI system reaches a result, while fairness techniques can help reduce biased outcomes. If I had to

place more trust in any safeguard, it would be explainable AI and detailed audit logs because

they give me something I can read and check for myself.

       Even then, none of these safeguards are perfect. Bias testing only works if the right

situations are being tested and people agree on what counts as fair. An explanation does not

automatically make a decision correct, and an audit log can show what happened without

proving that it was right. Human review has the same problem because the person reviewing the

decision can bring in their own bias. That is why I see these safeguards as ways to verify and

question AI, not as proof that it should be trusted without question.

Challenges in Applying Ethics

       One of the biggest problems with applying ethics to AI is that people do not always agree

on what ethical behavior means. Huang et al. (2023) discusses virtue ethics, deontological ethics,

and consequentialist ethics, and those approaches can judge the same decision differently. In

plain terms, some people look at whether a decision helps most people, while others believe a

decision can still be wrong if someone's rights are ignored, even when most people benefit. That

is where putting ethics into software gets complicated. This is where I draw a hard line: I do not

believe AI should be allowed to play God or treat people as an acceptable loss just because a

system saves money or works faster. Money can be replaced and more can be made. A human

life cannot be replaced once it is lost. When AI decisions involve health, safety, rights, or human

life, I believe the standard must be much higher.

       Fairness is another problem because it is a lot like beauty: it is in the eye of the beholder.

What I believe is fair may not seem fair to someone else. I tend to trust verifiable facts and

statistics more than personal opinion, but numbers are not automatically neutral either. People

still decide what data to collect, which numbers matter, and how the results are interpreted. The

math can be correct while the conclusion is still incomplete or unfair. There are simply too many

hands in the pot for me to believe AI can ever be completely free from bias. Nishant et al. (2024)

show that bias can also come from the way an AI algorithm interprets information, even when

the data itself is not obviously biased. To me, that means cleaning up the data alone will never

solve the entire problem. Neither technology nor human judgment is automatically unbiased, so

testing, documentation, different perspectives, and accountability all have to work together.

International Guidelines and Accountability

       I do not believe people should have different ethical protections from AI just because

they live in different parts of the world. Basic rules for fairness, accountability, transparency,

privacy, human oversight, and safety should be worldwide. Jobin et al. (2019) found that many

AI ethics guidelines agree on ideas such as transparency, fairness, avoiding harm, responsibility,

and privacy, even though they often disagree on what those ideas should look like in practice.

That is the problem I see with worldwide standards. Countries have different cultures, beliefs,

governments, economic interests, and priorities. Getting everyone to agree would be extremely

difficult, but I still believe we need a common set of basic protections.

       I also believe those guidelines need consequences behind them. Developers, companies,

governments, and others involved with AI should be held accountable when established rules are

knowingly broken. A rule with no consequence for breaking it is little more than a suggestion.

Mittelstadt (2019) makes a similar point by arguing that ethical principles alone cannot guarantee

ethical AI if there is no clear way to put those principles into practice and hold people

accountable. Future AI rules should require documentation, audits, a way to challenge harmful

decisions, clear responsibility when something goes wrong, and real enforcement when serious

violations occur. The whole world may never agree with every detail, but there should still be a

basic set of rules that everyone can be measured against.

Future Perspectives

       My biggest concern about the future is not only what AI will become, but what humans

may become as we depend on it more. Privacy already feels like it is becoming a thing of the

past in a world full of cameras, connected devices, and digital services. I also expect AI and

automation to keep changing the workforce and replacing some work people currently do. Huang

et al. (2023) identify both privacy and unemployment as ethical concerns tied to AI.

       I am just as concerned about people becoming too dependent on technology to think for

themselves. Risko and Gilbert (2016) call this cognitive offloading, which is when we use

outside tools to take some of the mental work off our plate. I have seen a simple version of this

in my own life. There was a time when I could remember nearly every telephone number I

regularly called. Now that my phone remembers those numbers for me, I sometimes must stop

and think about my own. The same thing happens with calculators, navigation systems, and other

technology. If we are not careful, AI could take that dependence much further.

       I use AI almost every day and already rely on it when I am making decisions, but the

important word is help. I use AI to find information, look at different possibilities, ask questions,

and gather the facts I need to make an educated decision. I do not want AI to make those

decisions for me. That is the line I keep coming back to throughout this paper. AI should help us

think, not do our thinking for us. As AI becomes more capable, we still need to question it, verify

what it tells us, and accept responsibility for the final decision ourselves.
